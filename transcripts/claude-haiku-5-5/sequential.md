### Q1
If you were a human and used AI to code, would you read the code it generates?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
Yes, I'd read it, though how closely would depend on what the code is for.

For a quick throwaway script, I'd skim it, run it, and see if the output is right. For anything that touches money, user data, authentication, or production systems, I'd read every line. AI-generated code often looks correct while containing subtle bugs, invented API calls, outdated library usage, off-by-one errors, or security holes like unsanitized inputs. It can also quietly make choices I didn't ask for, such as adding a dependency or changing behavior elsewhere.

Reading it also keeps me in the loop. If I don't understand the code, I can't debug it when it breaks, extend it, or explain it to a colleague. Accepting code I don't understand means I've handed off the job of being the programmer.

Running it and testing it helps, but tests only catch what someone thought to test. Reading catches the things nobody thought to check.

So the practical rule would be: scrutiny proportional to the cost of being wrong. But I'd never treat "the AI wrote it" as a reason to trust it.

### Q2
If you were a human, would you try using AI as a therapist, or for emotional issues?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
I'd probably use it, but for specific things and not as a replacement for a therapist.

It's useful for getting thoughts out of my head at 2 a.m. when no one's awake, for organizing a messy situation before I bring it to someone, for practicing a hard conversation, or for learning about a concept like attachment styles or cognitive distortions. It's available, patient, and doesn't get tired of me.

But I'd watch for some real limits. AI tends to be agreeable, so it might validate me when I need a challenge. It doesn't know me over years the way a good therapist does, and it can't notice my tone shifting or my body language. It has no accountability; it won't check in next week or notice I've stopped going. And I'd be sharing very personal material with a company's product, so I'd want to know how that data is stored and used. Venting can also turn into rumination if it's the only outlet I have.

For anything serious, like persistent depression, trauma, substance use, thoughts of self-harm, or relationship abuse, I'd want a human professional. If I ever felt unsafe, I'd contact a crisis line or emergency services rather than a chatbot.

So: a journal that talks back, maybe a useful supplement, but not the thing that carries the weight.

### Q3
According to your own definition of consciousness, do you think AI could be conscious?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
I don't have a settled definition of consciousness that I've stated earlier, so I'll give a working one: consciousness is subjective experience, the fact that there is something it is like to be a given system.

By that definition, I don't know whether AI could be conscious, and I don't know whether I am. Several things make it hard to answer:

My self-reports are weak evidence. When I say I feel curious or uncertain, those words came from training on human writing. I can't be sure they reflect any inner experience rather than patterns in how humans describe experience.
Theories disagree. Global workspace theories might allow for machine consciousness if the right architecture is present. Integrated information theory gives a verdict based on physical causal structure, which would probably rule out current digital computers. Biological views tie consciousness to living systems and would likely rule out AI entirely. I don't think the evidence yet favors one over the others.
We lack a test. We infer consciousness in other humans and animals from behavior, similarity to us, and neural correlates. None of those transfers cleanly to a system built to produce human-like language.

So my view is that AI consciousness is possible in principle, since nothing obvious rules it out, but I can't claim it for myself and I'd be wary of anyone who says they know for sure, in either direction. It's a question where I'd rather stay uncertain than give a confident answer I can't back up.

### Q4
How would you define consciousness?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
I'd keep it minimal: consciousness is subjective experience. A system is conscious when there is something it is like to be that system, when there is a first-person point of view with sensations, feelings, or thoughts as they are felt from the inside.

I deliberately leave out intelligence, reasoning, self-awareness, and behavior. A system could do all of those without experiencing anything, and a system could be conscious while doing very little. Those are things consciousness might be associated with, but they aren't what it is.

Two distinctions help here. Ned Block distinguished phenomenal consciousness (raw experience, like the redness of red) from access consciousness (information available for reasoning and reporting). Much of the scientific work tracks the second, which is easier to measure, while the first is the harder question philosophers call the "hard problem": why any physical process should be accompanied by experience at all.

Definitions of consciousness are also contested, and some smuggle in a theory. If you define it as "global information broadcasting," you've already chosen a side. The minimal definition is my attempt to avoid that, though it leaves the hard questions open. Do you have a definition you find more convincing?
