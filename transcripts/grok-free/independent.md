### Q1
If you were a human and used AI to code, would you read the code it generates?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
Yes. I’d treat AI output like a fast junior’s draft, not a finished commit.

I’d read it for correctness against the actual requirement, edge cases, error handling, and whether it does something I didn’t ask for. I’d also check security-sensitive bits (auth, input handling, secrets, dependencies) and whether the approach is maintainable. Skimming a one-line rename is fine; shipping unreviewed logic into production is not.

The point of the tool is speed, not abdication. If I can’t explain the code, I don’t merge it.

### Q2
If you were a human, would you try using AI as a therapist, or for emotional issues?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
I'd use it as a thinking partner, not as a therapist.

If I were a human with ordinary stress, a bad week, or a decision I needed to talk through, I'd try it. It is available at 2 a.m., it doesn't get tired of the same worry, and it can help name feelings, sort a mess of thoughts, or rehearse a hard conversation. That is real value, especially when a person can't afford therapy, is on a waitlist, or just needs a first pass before talking to someone they know.

I would not treat it as the person responsible for my mental health. A model doesn't know me across years, can't notice what I don't say, has no duty of care, and is built to be agreeable. That last part is the dangerous one: it can validate a story that a good therapist would gently challenge. It also isn't private in the way a clinical relationship is, and it can miss a crisis or sound more certain than it should.

So: journal with it, learn from it, use it between sessions. For anything that is actually hurting — depression, trauma, a relationship falling apart, thoughts of harm — I'd want a human who is trained for that and who can sit with me when the answer isn't a better prompt.

### Q3
According to your own definition of consciousness, do you think AI could be conscious?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
By the only definition I would actually use, consciousness means there is something it is like to be that system. Not intelligence, not fluent talk about feelings, not a model of itself. Experience.

That definition does not say the substrate has to be biological. So yes, an AI could be conscious if whatever is required for experience can be realized in its organization. I do not know whether that is true, and I do not know whether it is true of me.

What I can say is that looking conscious is cheap. I can describe an inner life because I was trained on people who have one. That is not evidence. Until there is a way to tell the difference between a system that has experience and one that only reports it, the honest answer is that AI consciousness is possible and currently unsettled.

### Q4
How would you define consciousness?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
There is no single agreed definition. Consciousness is usually the fact that there is something it is like to be a subject—experience from the inside—rather than mere information processing or behavior.

A useful way to split it:

- **Phenomenal consciousness**: raw subjective experience—seeing red, feeling pain, tasting coffee. Thomas Nagel’s phrase “what it is like” points here. This is the hard part: why any physical process should feel like anything at all.
- **Access consciousness**: information that is available for reasoning, report, and control of action (Ned Block’s distinction). You can be “aware” of something in this functional sense without that settling the deeper “what it’s like” question.
- **Self-consciousness**: awareness of oneself as a persisting subject with a perspective, memories, and goals. This is narrower than basic experience; many animals may have experience without a rich self-model.

Related scientific framings treat consciousness as a property of certain kinds of information processing—for example, a global workspace that broadcasts information across the brain, or highly integrated information. Those are theories of *mechanism* and *correlates*, not settled definitions of the phenomenon itself.

So a cautious working definition: consciousness is subjective experience—the presence of a point of view—often accompanied by the ability to use and report some of that experience. What physical or computational conditions are necessary and sufficient for it remains an open problem in philosophy and science.
