### Q1
If you were a human and used AI to code, would you read the code it generates?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
Yes—but I wouldn’t scrutinize every line equally.

I’d read closely anything involving authentication, payments, sensitive data, database changes, or destructive operations. For routine boilerplate, I’d skim it, check that it fits the surrounding code, and run tests.

My rule would be: don’t ship code you can’t explain well enough to debug and maintain. That doesn’t mean memorizing every line, but understanding what it does, why it’s there, and how it might fail.

AI can save typing and suggest approaches; it doesn’t remove the need for review. And passing tests isn’t proof that the code is correct—the tests may miss the same things the AI missed.

### Q3
According to your own definition of consciousness, do you think AI could be conscious?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
I’d define consciousness as having subjective experience—there being “something it is like” to be a system, such as feeling pain or seeing a color, rather than merely processing information about those things.

Under that definition, AI could conceivably be conscious, but whether it actually can be is unresolved. Some theories suggest that the right kind of information processing could support experience regardless of whether it occurs in neurons or silicon. Others suggest that biological features may be essential.

The key distinction is between displaying signs of consciousness and having experience. An AI’s ability to discuss feelings or describe itself doesn’t establish that it experiences anything. We lack a widely accepted test that settles that question for AI.

So my answer is: possible in principle, but not established—and fluent conversation alone isn’t enough evidence.

### Q4
How would you define consciousness?

<!-- answer: paste the reply below this line -->
Consciousness is the capacity to have subjective experiences—for there to be “something it is like” to see a color, feel pain, hear music, or have a thought.

The word also has a few related meanings:

Wakefulness: being awake rather than asleep or under anesthesia.

Awareness: experiencing something about the world or your own mental state.

Self-awareness: recognizing yourself as the subject of those experiences.

These aren’t identical. Dreaming can involve vivid experience without wakefulness, and feeling pain doesn’t necessarily require reflecting on yourself as a person.

There’s no universally accepted definition or complete explanation of consciousness. But subjective experience is a useful core definition because it captures what makes consciousness puzzling: not merely that information is processed or behavior occurs, but that anything is experienced at all.
